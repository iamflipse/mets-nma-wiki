Metabolic Syndrome: The 6 Components

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of cardiometabolic risk factors that travel together and independently raise the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and premature mortality. The diagnostic components this review tracks as primary outcomes:

ComponentAbbrev.Direction favoring benefit
Waist circumferenceWC↓
Systolic blood pressureSBP↓
Diastolic blood pressureDBP↓
Fasting blood glucoseFBG↓
TriglyceridesTG↓
HDL-cholesterolHDL-C↑ (the only component where higher is better)

Target population: adults (≥18 y) with MetS and overweight/obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m²) — excess adiposity is a major modifiable contributor to the cardiometabolic abnormalities of MetS, so weight management is a central therapeutic target.

Why component-specific comparisons matter: a treatment can improve one diagnostic component while leaving others unchanged, so an apparent "overall benefit" can mask clinically important outcome-specific differences. This is why the review compares each intervention node against each of the 6 outcomes separately rather than a single composite.

Lifestyle modification is the foundation of MetS management per major professional societies. Structured exercise-only interventions vary in methods, intensity, volume, supervision, and progression; dietary-only interventions vary in energy targets, macronutrient composition, and pattern. The 8 dietary nodes are grouped descriptively as:

  • Energy-deficit strategies: caloric restriction (CR), intermittent fasting (IF)
  • Single-macronutrient approaches: low-carbohydrate diet (LCD), high-protein diet (HPD), low-fat diet (LFD)
  • Whole-diet pattern interventions: Mediterranean diet (MedDiet), DASH, general dietary counseling (GDC)

Exercise nodes: MICT (moderate-intensity continuous training), HIIT (high-intensity interval training), RT (resistance training), COMB-EX (combined aerobic + resistance).

Bayesian NMA: How the Network Was Modeled

Registration: PROSPERO CRD420251134142.

Evidence base: 5 databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science, Scopus), inception → December 2025. 34,949 records → 78 RCTs, 193 intervention arms, 8,452 participants (median study duration 16.0 wk, IQR 12–24 wk; mean baseline BMI 31.6 kg/m²). 53–58 studies contributed to each outcome-specific network (53/54 for the 6 primary outcomes).

Bayesian network meta-analysis (primary method)

  • Software: R 4.5.2 with gemtc 1.1.0 + rjags 4.17; arm-level mean changes with SEs as data format.
  • Model: outcome-specific networks, normal likelihood, identity link, common between-study τ.
  • Priors: relative effect d_k ~ N(0, 10²) (vague normal); τ ~ Uniform(0.01, 10), with prior-sensitivity check using τ ~ LogNormal(−2.01, 1.64).
  • Inference: posterior median MDs with 95% credible intervals (CrIs); SUCRA for ranking.
  • Transitivity: indirect contrasts only as meaningful when trials are comparable in participant characteristics, baseline risk, duration, supervision, adherence, and outcome measurement.

Frequentist component NMA (auxiliary)

  • Software: netmeta 3.2.0.
  • Purpose: test whether the effect of a combined exercise–dietary intervention equals the sum of its exercise + diet component effects (additivity). Additivity tested by comparing the standard NMA model vs the additive CNMA with a Q-statistic difference test (Holm–Bonferroni corrected).
  • CNMA results reported as MDs with 95% CIs; component effects interpreted only when the additive model fits.

Certainty: CINeMA framework

Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis ratings (high/moderate/low/very low) applied per outcome, accounting for indirectness, imprecision, and risk of bias. Most comparisons rated low or very low.

Certainty, Bias, and Limitations

CINeMA certainty

  • Low to very low for most outcome-level comparisons, driven by imprecision, indirectness, and limited direct evidence.
  • The MICT-SBP (k=11, n=375), COMB-EX-DBP (k=9, n=441), and RT-FBG (k=7, n=127) nodes had direct control links, but outcome-level CINeMA summaries for SBP, DBP, and FBG were low certainty.

Key limitations (Discussion)

  1. Sparse combined nodes: many combined-intervention nodes relied on few trials and mostly indirect evidence; active-vs-active comparisons are less certain than intervention-vs-control.
  2. Additivity restricted: the CNMA only tested the additive specification; interaction terms were not estimated, so the rejected additivity for SBP/DBP/FBG/HDL-C indicates model misfit but doesn't identify which component pairs are synergistic or antagonistic.
  3. Duration: median study duration 16 wk → estimates mainly reflect short-to-medium-term effects; HDL-C changes may be under-detected.
  4. Eligibility: adults with MetS and BMI ≥25 kg/m² → limits generalizability to MetS adults with lower BMI.
  5. Harmonization gaps: differences in supervision, adherence, diet composition, energy deficit, and background medication could not be fully harmonized.
  6. Sex-specificity: only 2 trials reported sex-stratified baselines → no meaningful sex-specific synthesis or sex-by-intervention interaction.

Research agenda (Conclusion)

The evidence — mostly low/very low certainty, with few direct active comparisons — identifies which comparisons warrant adequately powered, longer-follow-up head-to-head trials:

  1. Direct testing of the favorable combined regimens (COMB-EX+MedDiet, MICT+CR, MICT+LCD) vs. single-component regimens.
  2. Longer trials to resolve HDL-C and sustained lipid effects.
  3. Consistent reporting of intervention delivery and adherence (supervision, fidelity).
  4. Sex-stratified outcomes (only 2/193 arms reported sex-specific baselines).

Divergence from earlier syntheses

Earlier comparative syntheses found multimodal interventions outperforming nutrition-only for MetS reversal; this review's more granular nodes (individual diagnostic components + specific regimens) produced outcome-specific patterns instead of a single "best" intervention. This aligns with a recent direct meta-analysis showing adding exercise to diet improved WC and FBG but not HDL-C, BP, or TG.

A B S T R A C T

Existing reviews have not clarified how exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise and dietary interventions compare across individual metabolic syndrome (MetS) components. We aimed to compare structured exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise–dietary interventions in adults with MetS and overweight or obesity with an inactive or minimal-intervention control or other active interventions for waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. We also assessed whether the combined effects were compatible with the sum of their component effects. We searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to December 2025 for randomized controlled trials. Comparative effects were estimated using Bayesian random-effects network meta-analysis; a frequentist component network meta-analysis was used to assess additivity, and certainty was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework. Seventy-eight trials with 8452 participants were included. Relative to the control node, all 4 structured exercise-only interventions were associated with lower SBP and DBP, and resistance training was associated with lower FBG. Among nodes representing combined exercise–dietary interventions, combined exercise plus Mediterranean diet, moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) plus caloric restriction, and MICT plus a low-carbohydrate diet were associated with lower WC, TG, and FBG, respectively, relative to the control node. For WC and TG, the results were consistent with a simple model that adds the estimated effects of exercise and diet; this model did not fit the other 4 outcomes. Certainty was low to very low for most comparisons. As a Bayesian synthesis, the findings do not identify a preferred intervention, but indicate which exercise, dietary, and combined approaches warrant testing in adequately powered direct comparative trials. This trial was registered at PROSPERO as CRD420251134142. Statement of significance

This review integrates Bayesian network and frequentist component network meta-analyses to compare structured exercise-only, dietaryonly, and combined interventions across 6 metabolic syndrome outcomes. The estimated effects varied by outcome, and adding the separate effects of exercise and diet did not consistently describe their combined effects. Together, these analyses provide an outcome-specific map of the evidence and identify comparisons for future head-to-head trials.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) enables simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions by integrating direct and indirect evidence [19]. Such inference depends on transitivity: trials contributing to different comparisons must be sufficiently comparable in participant characteristics, baseline risk, intervention duration, supervision, adherence, and outcome measurement for indirect contrasts to be meaningful. Component NMA (CNMA) further separates multicomponent interventions into their constituent parts to assess whether the observed effects of combined exercise–dietary interventions are compatible with an additive component structure [20,21]. This review of RCTs in adults with MetS and overweight or obesity aimed to compare structured exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise–dietary interventions with an inactive or minimal-intervention control or other active interventions for waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), TG, HDL-C, and secondary cardiometabolic outcomes. Bayesian NMA was used to estimate network contrasts, whereas CNMA was used to assess whether combined exercise–dietary interventions might have an additive effect.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic risk factors, including central adiposity, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL-C levels, hypertension, and insulin resistance [1–4], and is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and premature mortality [5]. Lifestyle modification is the foundation of MetS management and is recommended by major professional societies and guidelines [6–8]. Although overweight or obesity is not required for a MetS diagnosis, excess adiposity is common in MetS and is a major modifiable contributor to its cardiometabolic abnormalities. Therefore, we focused on adults with MetS and overweight or obesity to evaluate a clinically coherent high-adiposity phenotype for whom weight management is a central therapeutic target [6,9]. Behavioral lifestyle intervention is not a single, uniform treatment. Structured exercise-only interventions vary in methods, intensity, volume, supervision, and progression, whereas dietary-only interventions differ in energy targets, macronutrient composition, food patterns, behavioral support, and adherence requirements. These differences may produce distinct effects between coexisting but clinically separable components of MetS. Therefore, component-specific comparisons remain relevant even when an intervention improves overall metabolic health, because an effect on 1 component does not necessarily imply a comparable effect on the other components. A treatment may improve 1 diagnostic component while leaving others largely unchanged; an apparent overall benefit can consequently mask clinically important outcome-specific differences. Recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in adults with MetS have revealed variation in the effects of different interventions. Interval and continuous aerobic exercise training have produced different cardiometabolic responses [10], and systematic reviews indicate that exercise training can improve several MetS indicators [11]. An energy-restricted Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) combined with physical activity has been shown to improve adiposity and several metabolic components [12], and time-restricted eating can improve glycemic control [13]. Low-carbohydrate and MedDiet strategies have been associated with changes in anthropometric and metabolic outcomes [14,15], and intermittent fasting (IF) can reduce obesity, triglyceride (TG) levels, and insulin resistance in adults with MetS [16]. Combined exercise–dietary interventions are widely used because they address both energy expenditure and dietary intake and may have complementary effects. However, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have generally evaluated either structured exercise-only interventions or dietary-only interventions and have not considered combined exercise–dietary interventions or pooled diverse lifestyle interventions [11, 16–18]. Search strategy and eligibility criteria

Systematic searches were conducted in 5 electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, and Scopus) from inception to December 2025, using controlled vocabulary and free-text keywords related to MetS and structured exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise–dietary interventions. Dietary-only interventions were classified as caloric restriction (CR), MedDiet, low-carbohydrate diet (LCD), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), IF, high-protein diet (HPD), low-fat diet (LFD), or general dietary counseling (GDC) according to the predominant dietary approach reported in each arm. Study selection and data collection

Records were screened independently by 2 reviewers (JC and YQ) at the title/abstract and full-text stages. Eligibility was assessed against the PICOS criteria specified for this review. Reports referring to the same study were linked to avoid duplicate inclusion. Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate using a standardized form, and disagreements were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. For each eligible study, data were extracted on participant characteristics, interventions, comparators, outcomes, supervision, adherence, risk-of-bias (RoB) factors, and potential effect modifiers. Sex-stratified baseline participant characteristics were extracted by diet or intervention group when reported, retaining the original summary metric (mean ± SD or median and IQR) and distinguishing group-specific from overall sex-specific values. For continuous outcomes, baseline and postintervention means, SDs, SEs, and reported change values were extracted. Outcomes were analyzed as mean changes from baseline to endpoint, coded as endpoint minus baseline. When these could not be calculated directly, extracted change means were used. If a baseline or endpoint SD was missing but an SE was available, the SD was back-calculated as SE × sqrt(n). Change-score SDs were estimated as sqrt(SD_baseline2 + SD_endpoint2 − 2r × SD_baseline × SD_endpoint), assuming r = 0.5, and change-score SEs were calculated as SD_change/sqrt(n). No confidence interval (CI)–based conversions were used. Outcomes were stored in common clinical units. Body weight was treated as a secondary outcome and was not used to classify intervention nodes. Trials in which a drug was the primary intervention were excluded. Medication-use variables were extracted for descriptive purposes but were not included in formal transitivity testing or meta-regression because completeness was limited and coding was not sufficiently harmonized between trials.

Risk of bias in individual studies

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for randomized trials [27], independently and in duplicate, by each of the 2 primary reviewers.

Summary measures and synthesis

All 6 primary outcomes were evaluated using NMA, CNMA, inconsistency assessment, meta-regression, sensitivity analysis, assessment of publication bias, and certainty evaluation. Outcome-specific networks were analyzed separately after values were converted to common clinical units. The mean difference (MD) was used as the metric because each network related to the same outcome construct. Estimates were not standardized between different outcomes. Bayesian results were reported as posterior median MDs with 95% credible intervals (CrIs), whereas CNMA results were reported as MDs with 95% CIs. Bayesian random-effects NMA was conducted in R version 4.5.2 with gemtc 1.1.0 and rjags 4.17; frequentist analyses used netmeta 3.2.0. Each outcome-specific consistency model used a normal likelihood and identity link with a common between-study heterogeneity parameter (τ). Data were entered in arm-level format as mean changes from baseline with SEs. The relative effect of intervention k compared with the reference control (d_k) was assigned a vague normal (0, 102) prior. The primary heterogeneity prior was τ ~ uniform (0.01, 10), and the prior-sensitivity analysis used τ ~ lognormal (− 2.01, 1.64) [28]. Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling used 4 chains, 5000 adaptation iterations, 50,000 sampling iterations per chain, and thinning of 10. Results

Component NMA

CNMA was performed as a frequentist auxiliary analysis in netmeta, distinct from the primary Bayesian NMA. Additivity was tested by comparing the standard NMA model against the additive CNMA using the Q-statistic difference test with Holm–Bonferroni correction. Under the additive specification, the estimated effect of a combined exercise–dietary intervention was modeled as the sum of the exercise and dietary components. Component effects were interpreted only when the additive model provided an acceptable fit to the data [18]. The additivity assumption was formally tested for each primary outcome by comparing the standard network model with the additive component model.

Study selection

Database and registry searches identified 34,949 records. After removing 8250 duplicate records, 26,699 records were screened, and 24,553 were excluded. Of the 2146 reports sought for retrieval, 368 were not retrieved, and 1778 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Seventy-eight randomized trials were included in the systematic review (Figure 1); 53 to 58 studies contributed to the 6 outcome-specific primary NMA (Figure 2).

Study characteristics

The 78 RCTs included 8452 participants and 193 intervention arms. The median study-level intervention duration was 16.0 wk (IQR, 12.0–24.0 wk), and the participant-weighted mean baseline BMI was 31.6 kg/m2. Trial characteristics [10, 12,13,35–109] are summarized in Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3. Biological-sex data were available for 188 of 193 study arms (Supplemental Table 3A). Discussion This synthesis identifies outcome-specific patterns in controlreferenced estimates without establishing a consistently most favorable combined intervention. This outcome-specific pattern differs from earlier comparative syntheses in which broad lifestyle interventions ranked most highly for MetS reversal and multimodal interventions outperformed nutritional interventions, with advantages over exercise alone for some outcomes [110,111]. It is more consistent with a recent direct meta-analysis in which adding exercise to a dietary intervention improved WC and FBG but not HDL-C, blood pressure, or TG [112]. Differences in outcome definitions and intervention classification may explain part of this divergence: earlier reviews focused on MetS reversal or broad multimodal categories, whereas the present NMA evaluated individual diagnostic components and more specific intervention nodes. Within the network, the amount of evidence varied as much as the estimates themselves. The MICT-SBP, COMB-EX-DBP, and RT-FBG nodes drew on 11, 9, and 7 studies, with 375, 441, and 127 participants in the corresponding intervention arms, respectively. These nodes had direct links to control, whereas the outcome-level CINeMA summaries for SBP, DBP, and FBG were low certainty. In contrast, the favorable WC estimate for COMB-EX plus MedDiet and the FBG estimate for MICT plus LCD each arose from 1 trial, with 190 and 10 participants in the respective intervention arms. A major strength of this review is the comparison of structured exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined

exercise–dietary interventions within a unified network of 78 RCTs, 193 intervention arms, and 8452 participants. Bayesian NMA was complemented by CNMA, dose–response analysis, transitivity and inconsistency assessments, sensitivity analyses, and CINeMA certainty ratings. This combination of methods allowed the intervention patterns to be examined both by complete regimen and by individual component, as appropriate for the statistical analysis. Several features define the scope of these inferences. Many nodes representing combined exercise–dietary interventions were supported by few trials and relied heavily on indirect evidence, with the control node dominating the networks. Consequently, active-versus-active comparisons remain less certain than intervention-versus-control estimates. The auxiliary CNMA was restricted to the additive specification, and component-interaction terms were not estimated or used for inference. Therefore, rejection of the additivity assumption for SBP, DBP, FBG, and HDL-C indicates model misfit but does not identify specific synergistic or antagonistic component combinations, especially for comparisons for which evidence of the effects of individual combinations was sparse. Certainty was low or very low for most comparisons, primarily because of imprecision, indirectness, and limited direct evidence, and funnel-plot asymmetry raises concern about publication bias for TG and HDL-C. With a median study-level duration of 16 wk, these estimates mainly concern short-to-medium-term outcomes. Eligibility was restricted to adults with MetS and BMI ≥25 kg/m2, limiting generalizability to adults with MetS and lower BMI. Differences in supervision, adherence, diet composition, energy deficit, implementation fidelity, and background medication could not be fully harmonized [122]. Only 2 trials reported any sex-stratified baseline characteristics, and neither reported the full set of reviewer-requested traits. This precluded a meaningful sex-specific synthesis or within-study sex-by-intervention interaction assessment. Study-level meta-regression using the proportion of female participants cannot establish individual-level sex effects, and category averages may obscure clinically important variation among individual regimens. Taken together, this review maps the current comparative evidence across MetS components and identifies where direct trials would be most informative. Repeated exercise-versuscontrol comparisons provide a more established blood pressure signal, whereas RT for FBG and selected combined regimens for WC and TG raise further questions for activecomparator trials. These patterns do not establish an intervention hierarchy or demonstrate a general advantage of combined over single-component regimens. The predominantly low or very low certainty of the evidence and limited direct activecomparator evidence constrain their immediate relevance to treatment selection and patient care. The main contribution is a focused research agenda: to test the identified interventionoutcome signals in adequately powered head-to-head trials with longer follow-up and consistent reporting of intervention delivery and adherence. In conclusion, this review brings exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise–dietary interventions into a common, outcome-specific assessment of MetS components. Structured exercise-only interventions repeatedly yielded favorable control-referenced estimates for SBP and DBP; RT for FBG and selected combined regimens for WC and TG produced further

signals warranting direct testing. The CNMA was compatible with additivity for WC and TG, whereas the additive model did not fit the data for SBP, DBP, FBG, or HDL-C. The current evidence, mostly of low or very low certainty and with few direct active comparisons, does not establish a preferred regimen or a general advantage of combined interventions in patient care. Its contribution is to identify comparisons and evidence gaps that should shape adequately powered trials with longer follow-up.
