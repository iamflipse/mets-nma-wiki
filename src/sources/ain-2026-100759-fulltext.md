A B S T R A C T

Existing reviews have not clarified how exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise and dietary interventions compare across individual metabolic syndrome (MetS) components. We aimed to compare structured exercise-only, dietary-only, and combined exercise–dietary interventions in adults with MetS and overweight or obesity with an inactive or minimal-intervention control or other active
interventions for waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting blood glucose (FBG),
triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. We also assessed whether the combined effects were compatible with the sum
of their component effects. We searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, and Scopus from
inception to December 2025 for randomized controlled trials. Comparative effects were estimated using Bayesian random-effects network
meta-analysis; a frequentist component network meta-analysis was used to assess additivity, and certainty was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework. Seventy-eight trials with 8452 participants were included. Relative to the control node, all 4
structured exercise-only interventions were associated with lower SBP and DBP, and resistance training was associated with lower FBG.
Among nodes representing combined exercise–dietary interventions, combined exercise plus Mediterranean diet, moderate-intensity
continuous training (MICT) plus caloric restriction, and MICT plus a low-carbohydrate diet were associated with lower WC, TG, and
FBG, respectively, relative to the control node. For WC and TG, the results were consistent with a simple model that adds the estimated
effects of exercise and diet; this model did not fit the other 4 outcomes. Certainty was low to very low for most comparisons. As a Bayesian
synthesis, the findings do not identify a preferred intervention, but indicate which exercise, dietary, and combined approaches warrant
testing in adequately powered direct comparative trials.
This trial was registered at PROSPERO as CRD420251134142.

Statement of significance

This review integrates Bayesian network and frequentist component network meta-analyses to compare structured exercise-only, dietaryonly, and combined interventions across 6 metabolic syndrome outcomes. The estimated effects varied by outcome, and adding the separate
effects of exercise and diet did not consistently describe their combined effects. Together, these analyses provide an outcome-specific map of the
evidence and identify comparisons for future head-to-head trials.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic risk factors,
including central adiposity, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL-C
levels, hypertension, and insulin resistance [1–4], and is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2
diabetes, and premature mortality [5]. Lifestyle modification is
the foundation of MetS management and is recommended by
major professional societies and guidelines [6–8]. Although
overweight or obesity is not required for a MetS diagnosis,
excess adiposity is common in MetS and is a major modifiable
contributor to its cardiometabolic abnormalities. Therefore, we
focused on adults with MetS and overweight or obesity to
evaluate a clinically coherent high-adiposity phenotype for
whom weight management is a central therapeutic target [6,9].
Behavioral lifestyle intervention is not a single, uniform
treatment. Structured exercise-only interventions vary in
methods, intensity, volume, supervision, and progression,
whereas dietary-only interventions differ in energy targets,
macronutrient composition, food patterns, behavioral support,
and adherence requirements. These differences may produce
distinct effects between coexisting but clinically separable
components of MetS. Therefore, component-specific comparisons remain relevant even when an intervention improves
overall metabolic health, because an effect on 1 component does
not necessarily imply a comparable effect on the other components. A treatment may improve 1 diagnostic component while
leaving others largely unchanged; an apparent overall benefit
can consequently mask clinically important outcome-specific
differences. Recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in
adults with MetS have revealed variation in the effects of
different interventions. Interval and continuous aerobic exercise
training have produced different cardiometabolic responses
[10], and systematic reviews indicate that exercise training can
improve several MetS indicators [11]. An energy-restricted
Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) combined with physical activity
has been shown to improve adiposity and several metabolic
components [12], and time-restricted eating can improve glycemic control [13]. Low-carbohydrate and MedDiet strategies
have been associated with changes in anthropometric and
metabolic outcomes [14,15], and intermittent fasting (IF) can
reduce obesity, triglyceride (TG) levels, and insulin resistance in
adults with MetS [16].
Combined exercise–dietary interventions are widely used
because they address both energy expenditure and dietary
intake and may have complementary effects. However, previous
systematic reviews and meta-analyses have generally evaluated
either structured exercise-only interventions or dietary-only
interventions and have not considered combined exercise–dietary interventions or pooled diverse lifestyle interventions [11,
16–18]. Moreover, they have not clearly separated comparisons

The review was registered at PROSPERO (CRD420251

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

Systematic searches were conducted in 5 electronic databases
(PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled
Trials, Web of Science, and Scopus) from inception to December
2025, using controlled vocabulary and free-text keywords
related to MetS and structured exercise-only, dietary-only, and
combined exercise–dietary interventions. The full strategies are
provided in Supplemental Table 1. The eligibility criteria followed the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and
Study Design (PICOS) framework (Table 1). In brief, articles
were required to report the results of RCTs in adults aged ≥18 y
with MetS and overweight or obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) that
tested structured exercise-only, dietary-only, or combined
exercise–dietary interventions of ≥4 wk duration and reported

≥1 prespecified cardiometabolic outcome. Randomized crossover trials were eligible under the registered criterion of randomized study designs; however, their contribution to an
outcome-specific NMA required an analyzable between-node
contrast. Overweight or obesity was prespecified in the original PROSPERO registration and defined the target population;
it was not treated as a diagnostic requirement for MetS.

Study selection and data collection

Records were screened independently by 2 reviewers (JC and
YQ) at the title/abstract and full-text stages. Eligibility was
assessed against the PICOS criteria specified for this review.
Reports referring to the same study were linked to avoid
duplicate inclusion. Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate using a standardized form, and disagreements were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a
third reviewer. For each eligible study, data were extracted on
participant characteristics, interventions, comparators, outcomes, supervision, adherence, risk-of-bias (RoB) factors, and
potential effect modifiers. Sex-stratified baseline participant
characteristics were extracted by diet or intervention group
when reported, retaining the original summary metric (mean ±
SD or median and IQR) and distinguishing group-specific from
overall sex-specific values. For continuous outcomes, baseline
and postintervention means, SDs, SEs, and reported change
values were extracted. Outcomes were analyzed as mean
changes from baseline to endpoint, coded as endpoint minus
baseline. When these could not be calculated directly, extracted
change means were used. If a baseline or endpoint SD was
missing but an SE was available, the SD was back-calculated as
SE × sqrt(n). Change-score SDs were estimated as sqrt(SD_baseline2 + SD_endpoint2 − 2r × SD_baseline × SD_endpoint),
assuming r = 0.5, and change-score SEs were calculated as
SD_change/sqrt(n). No confidence interval (CI)–based conversions were used. Outcomes were stored in common clinical
units. Body weight was treated as a secondary outcome and was
not used to classify intervention nodes. Trials in which a drug
was the primary intervention were excluded. Medication-use
variables were extracted for descriptive purposes but were not
included in formal transitivity testing or meta-regression
because completeness was limited and coding was not sufficiently harmonized between trials.

Risk of bias in individual studies

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for
randomized trials [27], independently and in duplicate, by each
of the 2 primary reviewers.

Summary measures and synthesis

All 6 primary outcomes were evaluated using NMA, CNMA,
inconsistency assessment, meta-regression, sensitivity analysis,
assessment of publication bias, and certainty evaluation.
Outcome-specific networks were analyzed separately after values
were converted to common clinical units. The mean difference
(MD) was used as the metric because each network related to the
same outcome construct. Estimates were not standardized between different outcomes. Bayesian results were reported as
posterior median MDs with 95% credible intervals (CrIs),
whereas CNMA results were reported as MDs with 95% CIs.

analysis used τ ~ lognormal (− 2.01, 1.64) [28]. Markov chain
Monte Carlo sampling used 4 chains, 5000 adaptation iterations,

Sensitivity analysis
Sensitivity analyses examined robustness to prior selection,
influential studies, sparse nodes, and the assumed baselineendpoint correlation used to derive change-score SDs. The primary analysis used r = 0.5, and the models were repeated using r
= 0.3 and r = 0.7. Substantial instability was defined as either a
marked shift in treatment ranking or a reversal in the direction
of effect.
Publication bias
Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using
comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger and Begg tests
[31–33].

Credibility assessment

Confidence in the evidence for the main comparisons was
assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis
(CINeMA) framework [34], considering within-study bias,
reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and
incoherence.

Results

Component NMA

CNMA was performed as a frequentist auxiliary analysis in
netmeta, distinct from the primary Bayesian NMA. Additivity
was tested by comparing the standard NMA model against the
additive CNMA using the Q-statistic difference test with
Holm–Bonferroni correction. Under the additive specification,
the estimated effect of a combined exercise–dietary intervention
was modeled as the sum of the exercise and dietary components.
Component effects were interpreted only when the additive
model provided an acceptable fit to the data [18]. The additivity
assumption was formally tested for each primary outcome by
comparing the standard network model with the additive
component model.

Study selection

Database and registry searches identified 34,949 records. After
removing 8250 duplicate records, 26,699 records were screened,
and 24,553 were excluded. Of the 2146 reports sought for
retrieval, 368 were not retrieved, and 1778 full-text reports were
assessed for eligibility. Seventy-eight randomized trials were
included in the systematic review (Figure 1); 53 to 58 studies
contributed to the 6 outcome-specific primary NMA (Figure 2).

Study characteristics

The 78 RCTs included 8452 participants and 193 intervention arms. The median study-level intervention duration was
16.0 wk (IQR, 12.0–24.0 wk), and the participant-weighted
mean baseline BMI was 31.6 kg/m2. Trial characteristics [10,
12,13,35–109] are summarized in Table 2 and Supplemental
Table 3. Biological-sex data were available for 188 of 193 study
arms (Supplemental Table 3A). Only 2 studies reported
extractable sex-disaggregated baseline characteristics. These
source-reported values are summarized without conversion or
pooling in Supplemental Table 3B; the available data were
insufficient for a sex-specific treatment-effect synthesis.

Discussion
This synthesis identifies outcome-specific patterns in controlreferenced estimates without establishing a consistently most
favorable combined intervention. This outcome-specific pattern
differs from earlier comparative syntheses in which broad lifestyle interventions ranked most highly for MetS reversal and
multimodal interventions outperformed nutritional interventions, with advantages over exercise alone for some outcomes [110,111]. It is more consistent with a recent direct
meta-analysis in which adding exercise to a dietary intervention improved WC and FBG but not HDL-C, blood pressure, or
TG [112]. Differences in outcome definitions and intervention
classification may explain part of this divergence: earlier reviews focused on MetS reversal or broad multimodal categories,
whereas the present NMA evaluated individual diagnostic
components and more specific intervention nodes.
Within the network, the amount of evidence varied as much
as the estimates themselves. The MICT-SBP, COMB-EX-DBP, and
RT-FBG nodes drew on 11, 9, and 7 studies, with 375, 441, and
127 participants in the corresponding intervention arms,
respectively. These nodes had direct links to control, whereas
the outcome-level CINeMA summaries for SBP, DBP, and FBG
were low certainty. In contrast, the favorable WC estimate for
COMB-EX plus MedDiet and the FBG estimate for MICT plus LCD
each arose from 1 trial, with 190 and 10 participants in the
respective intervention arms. The TG estimate for MICT plus CR
was based on 4 studies and 152 participants in those arms;

transitivity and inconsistency assessments, sensitivity analyses,
and CINeMA certainty ratings. This combination of methods
allowed the intervention patterns to be examined both by
complete regimen and by individual component, as appropriate
for the statistical analysis.
Several features define the scope of these inferences. Many
nodes representing combined exercise–dietary interventions
were supported by few trials and relied heavily on indirect evidence, with the control node dominating the networks.
Consequently, active-versus-active comparisons remain less
certain than intervention-versus-control estimates. The auxiliary CNMA was restricted to the additive specification, and
component-interaction terms were not estimated or used for
inference. Therefore, rejection of the additivity assumption for
SBP, DBP, FBG, and HDL-C indicates model misfit but does not
identify specific synergistic or antagonistic component combinations, especially for comparisons for which evidence of the
effects of individual combinations was sparse. Certainty was low
or very low for most comparisons, primarily because of imprecision, indirectness, and limited direct evidence, and funnel-plot
asymmetry raises concern about publication bias for TG and
HDL-C. With a median study-level duration of 16 wk, these estimates mainly concern short-to-medium-term outcomes. Eligibility was restricted to adults with MetS and BMI ≥25 kg/m2,
limiting generalizability to adults with MetS and lower BMI.
Differences in supervision, adherence, diet composition, energy
deficit, implementation fidelity, and background medication
could not be fully harmonized [122]. Only 2 trials reported any
sex-stratified baseline characteristics, and neither reported the
full set of reviewer-requested traits. This precluded a meaningful
sex-specific synthesis or within-study sex-by-intervention
interaction assessment. Study-level meta-regression using the
proportion of female participants cannot establish
individual-level sex effects, and category averages may obscure
clinically important variation among individual regimens.
Taken together, this review maps the current comparative
evidence across MetS components and identifies where direct
trials would be most informative. Repeated exercise-versuscontrol comparisons provide a more established blood pressure signal, whereas RT for FBG and selected combined regimens for WC and TG raise further questions for activecomparator trials. These patterns do not establish an intervention hierarchy or demonstrate a general advantage of combined
over single-component regimens. The predominantly low or
very low certainty of the evidence and limited direct activecomparator evidence constrain their immediate relevance to
treatment selection and patient care. The main contribution is a
focused research agenda: to test the identified interventionoutcome signals in adequately powered head-to-head trials
with longer follow-up and consistent reporting of intervention
delivery and adherence.
In conclusion, this review brings exercise-only, dietary-only,
and combined exercise–dietary interventions into a common,
outcome-specific assessment of MetS components. Structured
exercise-only interventions repeatedly yielded favorable
control-referenced estimates for SBP and DBP; RT for FBG and
selected combined regimens for WC and TG produced further

signals warranting direct testing. The CNMA was compatible
with additivity for WC and TG, whereas the additive model did
not fit the data for SBP, DBP, FBG, or HDL-C. The current evidence, mostly of low or very low certainty and with few direct
active comparisons, does not establish a preferred regimen or a
general advantage of combined interventions in patient care. Its
contribution is to identify comparisons and evidence gaps that
should shape adequately powered trials with longer follow-up.
